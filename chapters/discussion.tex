\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chp:discussion}

\section{Interpretation of results}
The central question behind this thesis is whether a single tool can bridge the gap between classroom exercise tools and controlled research software. The results from the classroom deployment provide a first answer. This section interprets the findings from Chapter~\ref{chp:results} along three dimensions: the tool as a research instrument, the engagement system, and the adaptive difficulty system.

\subsection*{Did the tool work as a research instrument?}
The short answer is yes. Two researchers with no programming background configured a 2$\times$2 factorial study with four conditions, deployed it across seven schools, ran it over four training days with 107 participants, monitored progress through the dashboard, and exported structured data ready for analysis. There were problems along the way (the duplicate submission bug, a server downtime incident, a demo that was too long) but none of these resulted in permanent data loss or forced the study to be abandoned. Eline's feedback confirms that the overall experience was a clear improvement over her previous work with OpenSesame and PsychoPy, particularly in terms of setup complexity and data accessibility. The final scientific results of the study are not yet available, as Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems will complete their master's thesis in the next academic year. But the fact that the tool carried a study of this scale to completion, producing 21,522 clean exercise records across four conditions, demonstrates that the gap identified in Chapters~\ref{chp:background} and~\ref{chp:exercise-tools} can be addressed in practice.

\subsection*{Did the engagement system deliver?}
The behavioural data suggests it did. Children spent 98.5\% of their earned coins on puzzle pieces, and 99\% completed at least one puzzle, all without any instruction or incentive to do so\footnote{Although the children were informed that puzzles were available and could be explored after each training session, they were never required, reminded, or externally rewarded to interact with them.}. If the gamification had been irrelevant to the children, we would expect most coins to go unspent. Instead, the near complete spending and the variation in purchasing strategies (cheap puzzles for quantity versus expensive ones for the challenge) point to genuine, voluntary engagement with the reward system. Eline's qualitative observations support this: children were visibly enthusiastic about rescuing animals and asked to continue playing. The important cavconcerneat is that engagement is not the same as learning. Research on gamification in education has consistently found positive effects on motivation and engagement, but the evidence for direct improvements in learning outcomes remains mixed \cite{sailer2020gamification}. The data shows that children were motivated to use the tool, but it cannot show whether that motivation translated into better arithmetic performance over the four training days. Establishing a causal link between gamification and learning outcomes would require a study designed specifically for that purpose, which was outside the scope of this thesis.

\subsection*{Did the adaptivity system perform as designed?}
Partially. The system succeeded at its primary goal: differentiating between children of different ability levels and adjusting the difficulty accordingly. Final Elo scores spread from 500 to 1000, the trajectory data shows the system responding individually to each child, and children in the adaptive conditions received exercises that were on average harder (mean difficulty 2.54) than those in the non-adaptive conditions (2.00). Where the system fell short is in its calibration. The intended target accuracy of 75\% was not reached; the adaptive group averaged 68\%, meaning exercises were somewhat too difficult for the average participant. The most likely causes are the fixed expected score and the quadratic scoring function (Equation~\ref{for:scoring-function}), both of which were calibrated on limited pilot data. A more refined approach would assign an Elo rating to each individual exercise rather than relying on predefined difficulty categories, following the item-level model used in Rekentuin \cite{klinkenberg2011computer}. The ceiling effect reinforces this point: 14 children reached the maximum rating of 1000, meaning the exercise pool could no longer challenge them. These are calibration and content problems, not architectural ones. The adaptive framework responds to performance, differentiates between children, and adjusts in real time. What it needs is better parameters and a deeper exercise pool.\ \
One final observation concerns response times. The adaptive group clustered toward longer response times, while the non-adaptive group was spread uniformly. This follows directly from the scoring function, whose equilibrium sits at the time limit. The pattern confirms that the system behaves as mathematically expected, but it raises the question of whether working at the edge of the time limit is pedagogically desirable. A scoring function with a more generous equilibrium might produce a more comfortable experience without sacrificing the adaptive mechanism.



\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
This section discusses the main limitations of RekenRangers as a tool and of the evaluation presented in this thesis. The limitations are grouped into two categories: limitations of the tool itself, and limitations of the evaluation.

\subsection{Limitations of the tool}
The most significant limitation of RekenRangers is its restricted flexibility. As anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:research-properties} and acknowledged in Section~\ref{sec:flexibility}, there is an inherent tension between making a tool accessible to non-programmers (i.e., ease of setup) and supporting the full range of experimental designs a researcher might want to run (i.e., flexibility) \cite{lidwell2010universal}. RekenRangers sits firmly on the accessibility side of that trade-off. The study by Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems was possible because the platform was developed with their specific experimental design in mind, but for studies that require a custom adaptive algorithm, a different stimulus modality, or trial structures outside the block-based model, RekenRangers offers no path forward. The tool is, in its current form, a research instrument for arithmetic studies that fit its built-in templates, not a general experiment builder. Addressing this without sacrificing the no-code principle is a non-trivial design problem and is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.\\ \\
A related limitation concerns the teacher and researcher interface. While the configuration is performed entirely through dropdown menus and checkboxes, the interface itself was not designed for the scale it now supports. As the number of options grew over the course of development, the structure of the settings screens did not scale with it, and the result is a panel that is functional but lacks the visual organisation a user might expect from a mature research tool. Eline's feedback specifically noted that researchers cannot see a preview of the experiment they are configuring. There is no equivalent of the structured experiment view that OpenSesame and PsychoPy offer. This is a real usability gap, and it limits the practical experience of setting up complex studies.\\ \\
Several of the twelve properties identified in Chapters~\ref{chp:background} and~\ref{chp:exercise-tools} are present but underdeveloped. Personalisation is the clearest example: the foundation for an avatar system, customisable profiles, and personal statistics is in place, but no visual personalisation was implemented within the scope of this thesis. The feedback system is similarly minimal, providing only correctness information without further explanation of why a wrong answer was wrong or what strategy the child could have used. Both extensions are realistic for future development and are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.\\ \\
Finally, there are smaller operational limitations worth mentioning. There is currently no way for a reseracher or teacher who has forgotten their password to recover it without contacting the administrator. This illustrates the kind of practical detail that becomes important when a tool moves from prototype to deployment.\\ \\

\subsection{Limitations of the evaluation}
The evaluation presented in this thesis has limitations of its own. The most significant is that the only researchers who used RekenRangers to run a real study were Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems, both of whom were closely involved in the design of the tool throughout its development. Their feedback is informative and detailed, but they are not naive first-time users. Some of the design choices in the platform were made specifically in response to their input, which means their experience is unlikely to be representative of what a researcher encountering the tool for the first time would find. A meaningful external evaluation, with researchers and teachers who had no prior contact with the project, would be necessary to confirm the conclusions drawn here.\\ \\
The thesis also does not provide direct evidence about learning outcomes. The data presented in Chapter~\ref{chp:results} shows that children engaged with the tool, that the adaptive system differentiated between ability levels, and that the platform collected complete and structured data. What it cannot show is whether children who used RekenRangers improved their arithmetic skills more than they would have using another tool, or whether the metacognitive prompts produced the intended effect on confidence calibration. These questions will be addressed in the scientific analysis carried out by Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems for their own thesis, but they fall outside the scope of the present work.\\ \\

\section{Collaborating across disciplines: reflections for a computer scientist}
Building a tool like RekenRangers in collaboration with educational scientists is a tougher challenge than I expected at the start of this thesis. Computer scientists are trained to think in terms of features, systems, and abstractions. Educational scientists are trained to think in terms of variables, conditions, and confounds. They see things that I would never have thought about, and they ask precise questions that often expose hidden assumptions in the design. Working with my co-supervisor Elien Bellon and with Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems was an enormous benefit to the quality of the tool: they grounded design decisions in actual research practice, they brought credibility to the project, and they pushed me to think more carefully than I would have on my own.\\ \\
It also made the development process considerably more iterative. Almost every part of RekenRangers, from the front-end interface to the adaptive algorithm, went through several rounds of feedback and compromise. The collaboration was rewarding but not always efficient.\\ \\
The tension worked in both directions. From my side, I sometimes underestimated how much detail educational research demands: a setting that seemed acceptable to me would turn out to have implications for confounding variables that I had not considered. From the other side, Elien, Eline, and Kiara naturally framed their requirements in terms of their own studies. My goal was to build a more general tool, which meant I often had to translate a specific feature request into a more abstract version that would also serve other researchers in the future. This was not always easy, and it required active pushback from my side to keep the tool from becoming a custom-built instrument for one experiment.\\ \\
Miscommunication occasionally led to wasted effort. The clearest example was a long struggle over the "randomness" of exercise selection. To me, random meant statistically random. To them, it meant varied in a way that avoided unwanted patterns. We spent weeks aligning on what the word actually meant before the implementation could be finalised. Small misalignments like this, when accumulated, slow the project down considerably.\\ \\
If I had to point to one lesson, it would be the importance of explicitly discussing the design goals up front, and revisiting them throughout the project. It is tempting to dive into features and implementation details from the start, but a few hours spent agreeing on what the tool is for, and for whom, would have saved many more hours of rework. Cross-disciplinary collaboration is genuinely valuable, but it requires both sides to keep their long-term goals visible to each other.
