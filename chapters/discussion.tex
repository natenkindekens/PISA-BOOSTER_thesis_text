\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chp:discussion}

This chapter interprets what the results of the classroom deployment mean for the central question of this thesis. It first works through the findings from Chapter~\ref{chp:results}, then sets out the limitations of both the tool and the evaluation, and closes with a personal reflection on building RekenRangers in collaboration with educational scientists.

\section{Interpretation of results}
\label{sec:interpretation-of-results}
The central question behind this thesis is whether a single tool can bridge the gap between classroom exercise tools and controlled research software. The results from the classroom deployment provide a first answer. This section interprets the findings from Chapter~\ref{chp:results} along four dimensions: whether the tool worked as a research instrument, how the adaptive difficulty system behaved, whether the engagement system delivered, and how the end users experienced the platform.

\paragraph{Did the tool work as a research instrument?}
The short answer is yes. Two researchers with no programming background configured a 2$\times$2 factorial study with four conditions, deployed it across seven schools, ran it over four training days with 107 participants, monitored progress through the dashboard, and exported structured data ready for analysis. There were problems along the way (the duplicate submission bug, a server downtime incident, a demo that was too long) but none of these resulted in permanent data loss or forced the study to be abandoned. The researchers' feedback confirms that the overall experience was a clear improvement over their previous work with OpenSesame and PsychoPy, particularly in terms of setup complexity and data accessibility. The final scientific results of the study are not yet available, as Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems will complete their master's thesis in the next academic year. But the fact that the tool carried a study of this scale to completion, producing 21,522 clean exercise records across four conditions, demonstrates that the gap identified in Chapters~\ref{chp:background} and~\ref{chp:exercise-tools} can be addressed in practice.

\paragraph{Did the adaptivity system perform as designed?}
Partially. The system succeeded at its primary goal: differentiating between children of different ability levels and adjusting the difficulty accordingly. Final Elo scores spread from 500 to 1000, the trajectory data shows the system responding individually to each child, and children in the adaptive conditions received exercises that were on average harder (mean difficulty 2.54) than those in the non-adaptive conditions (2.00). Where the system fell short is in its calibration. The intended target accuracy of 75\% was not reached; the adaptive group averaged 68\%, meaning exercises were somewhat too difficult for the average participant. This quantitative finding is echoed in the qualitative observations: children in the adaptive conditions asked why their exercises were so hard, which is the kind of reaction the 75\% target was meant to avoid. The most likely causes are the fixed expected score and the quadratic scoring function (Equation~\ref{for:scoring-function}), both of which were calibrated on limited pilot data. A more refined approach would assign an Elo rating to each individual exercise rather than relying on predefined difficulty categories, following the item-level model used in Rekentuin \cite{klinkenberg2011computer}.

A second calibration issue is visible in the distribution of final Elo scores. A clear peak appears around 600--650, which is the boundary between difficulty zones 3 and 4 (see Table~\ref{tab:difficulty-zones}). The trajectory plots confirm that many of these children were oscillating across this boundary rather than progressing through it. The most likely explanation is the size of the step between zone 3 and zone 4: in zone 3 only 10\% of presented exercises are difficult, while in zone 4 this jumps to 70\%. The moment a child's rating crosses 600, the proportion of difficult exercises increases sharply, the child gets more answers wrong, the scoring function pulls the rating back below 600, and the cycle repeats. A more gradual transition between zones, or a continuous mapping from rating to difficulty mix rather than a step function, would likely smooth this out.

A separate observation concerns response times. The adaptive group clustered toward longer response times while the non-adaptive group was spread uniformly, which is what the scoring function predicts when its equilibrium sits at the time limit. The pattern confirms that the system behaves as designed, but it raises the question of whether working at the edge of the time limit is pedagogically desirable. A scoring function with a more generous equilibrium might produce a more comfortable experience without sacrificing the adaptive mechanism.

A fourth observation concerns the rating ceiling. Fourteen children reached the maximum rating of 1000, meaning the exercise pool could no longer challenge them. This, the 600 plateau, and the 68\% accuracy are all calibration and content problems, not architectural ones. The adaptive framework responds to performance, differentiates between children, and adjusts in real time. What it needs is better parameters, a more gradual zone transition, and a deeper exercise pool, all of which are discussed under future work in Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.

\paragraph{Did the engagement system deliver?}
The behavioural data suggests it did. Children spent 98.5\% of their earned coins on puzzle pieces, and 99\% completed at least one puzzle, all without any instruction or incentive to do so\footnote{Although the children were informed that puzzles were available and could be explored after each training session, they were never required, reminded, or externally rewarded to interact with them.}. If the gamification had been irrelevant, most coins would have gone unspent; instead, the near-complete spending and the variation in purchasing strategies (cheap puzzles for quantity versus expensive ones for the challenge) point to genuine, voluntary engagement with the reward system. The researchers' qualitative observations support this: children were visibly enthusiastic about rescuing animals and asked to continue playing.

The important concern is that engagement is not the same as learning. Research on gamification in education has consistently found positive effects on motivation and engagement, but the evidence for direct improvements in learning outcomes remains mixed \cite{sailer2020gamification}. The data here shows that children were motivated, not that the motivation translated into better arithmetic. A first informal look at the training data is consistent with this caution: across the four days, average accuracy increased slightly and average response time decreased slightly, but no statistical testing was carried out, and to the eye both measures look fairly constant. Establishing a genuine causal link between gamification and learning outcomes would require a study designed specifically for that purpose, which was outside the scope of this thesis.

\paragraph{How did the end users experience RekenRangers?}
The questionnaire results paint a clear picture from the children's side. The three motivation items (Q1,Q2 and Q5) all received highly positive scores, with weighted means of 3.78, 1.62 (negatively phrased), and 3.36 respectively. Children clearly enjoyed working with RekenRangers and would want to use it again, which reinforces the behavioural engagement evidence from the coin and puzzle data. The user friendliness items (Q3 and Q4) scored similarly strongly: children understood what they had to do (mean 3.78) and rarely needed help to operate the tool (mean 1.84 on the negatively phrased item). The feedback item (Q6) also received a high score (3.31), suggesting that the simple correct/incorrect signal was clear enough for the children to interpret. The adaptivity item (Q7) is the most divided, with a mean of 2.49: a substantial portion of the children felt that the exercises did not match their level well. This aligns with the quantitative finding that the adaptive system did not converge on its 75\% accuracy target and was somewhat too challenging on average.\footnote{Due to logistic constraints during the deployment, the returned questionnaires could not be traced back to individual experimental conditions. As a result, it is not possible to compare the responses on Q7 between the adaptive and non-adaptive groups, which would have provided a sharper test of whether the perceived mismatch comes specifically from the adaptive conditions.} Together, the questionnaire results confirm what the behavioural data already suggested: children experienced RekenRangers as engaging and easy to use, and the main area for improvement remains the calibration of the adaptive system.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
This section discusses the main limitations in two parts: limitations of RekenRangers as a tool, and limitations of the evaluation presented in this thesis.
.
\subsection{Limitations of the tool}
The most significant limitation of RekenRangers is its restricted flexibility. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:flexibility}, there is an inherent tension between making a tool accessible to non-programmers (ease of setup) and supporting the full range of experimental designs a researcher might want to run (flexibility) \cite{lidwell2010universal}. RekenRangers sits firmly on the accessibility side of that trade-off. The study by Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems was possible because the platform was developed with their specific experimental design in mind, but for studies that require a custom adaptive algorithm, a different stimulus modality, or trial structures outside the block-based model, RekenRangers offers no path forward. The tool is, in its current form, a research instrument for arithmetic studies that fit its built-in templates, not a general experiment builder. Addressing this without sacrificing the no-code principle is a non-trivial design problem and is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.

A related limitation concerns the teacher and researcher interface. While the configuration is performed entirely through dropdown menus and checkboxes, the interface itself was not designed for the scale it now supports. As the number of options grew over the course of development, the structure of the settings screens did not scale with it, and the result is a panel that is functional but lacks the visual organisation a user might expect from a mature research tool. The researchers' feedback specifically noted that they cannot see a preview of the experiment they are configuring. There is no equivalent of the structured experiment view that OpenSesame and PsychoPy offer. This is a real usability gap, and it limits the practical experience of setting up complex studies.

Several of the twelve properties identified in Chapters~\ref{chp:background} and~\ref{chp:exercise-tools} are present but underdeveloped. Personalisation is the clearest example: the foundation for an avatar system, customisable profiles, and personal statistics is in place, but no visual personalisation was implemented within the scope of this thesis. The feedback system is deliberately minimal, providing only correctness information; as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:exercise-properties}, minimal feedback can be a sound choice, but extending it with worked-out explanations or strategy hints could still be valuable. Adaptivity, finally, works as a mechanism but is not yet well calibrated, as the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:interpretation-of-results} showed. All three are realistic targets for future development and are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.

Finally, there are smaller operational limitations worth mentioning. There is currently no way for a researcher or teacher who has forgotten their password to recover it without contacting the administrator. This illustrates the kind of practical detail that becomes important when a tool moves from prototype to deployment.

\subsection{Limitations of the evaluation}
The evaluation presented in this thesis has limitations of its own. The most significant is that the only researchers who used RekenRangers to run a real study were Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems, both of whom were closely involved in the design of the tool throughout its development. Their feedback is informative and detailed, but they are not first-time users. Some of the design choices in the platform were made specifically in response to their input, which means their experience is unlikely to be representative of what a researcher encountering the tool for the first time would find. A meaningful external evaluation, with researchers and teachers who had no prior contact with the project, would be necessary to generalize the conclusions drawn here.

The thesis also does not provide direct evidence about learning outcomes. The data presented in Chapter~\ref{chp:results} shows that children engaged with the tool, that the adaptive system differentiated between ability levels, and that the platform collected complete and structured data. What it cannot show is whether children who used RekenRangers improved their arithmetic skills more than they would have using another tool. Answering that question would require a dedicated study designed for that purpose, which falls outside the scope of the present work.

\section{Collaborating across disciplines: reflections for a computer scientist}
Building a tool like RekenRangers in collaboration with educational scientists is a tougher challenge than I expected at the start of this thesis. Computer scientists are trained to think in terms of features, systems, and abstractions. Educational scientists are trained to think in terms of variables, conditions, and confounds. They see things that I would never have thought about, and they ask precise questions that often expose hidden assumptions in the design. Working with my co-supervisor Elien Bellon and with Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems was an enormous benefit to the quality of the tool: they grounded design decisions in actual research practice, they brought credibility to the project, and they pushed me to think more carefully than I would have on my own.

It also made the development process considerably more iterative. Almost every part of RekenRangers, from the front-end interface to the adaptive algorithm, went through several rounds of feedback and compromise. The collaboration was rewarding but not always efficient.

The tension worked in both directions. From my side, I sometimes underestimated how much detail educational research demands: a setting that seemed acceptable to me would turn out to have implications for confounding variables that I had not considered. From the other side, Elien, Eline, and Kiara naturally framed their requirements in terms of their own studies. My goal was to build a more general tool, which meant I often had to translate a specific feature request into a more abstract version that would also serve other researchers in the future. This was not always easy, and it required active pushback from my side to keep the tool from becoming a custom-built instrument for one experiment.

Miscommunication occasionally led to wasted effort. The clearest example was a long struggle over the "randomness" of exercise selection. To me, random meant statistically random. To them, it meant varied in a way that avoided unwanted patterns. We spent weeks aligning on what the word actually meant before the implementation could be finalised. Small misalignments like this, when accumulated, slow the project down considerably.

If I had to point to one lesson, it would be the importance of explicitly discussing the design goals up front, and revisiting them throughout the project. It is tempting to dive into features and implementation details from the start, but a few hours spent agreeing on what the tool is for, and for whom, would have saved many more hours of rework. Cross-disciplinary collaboration is genuinely valuable, but it requires both sides to keep their long-term goals visible to each other.
