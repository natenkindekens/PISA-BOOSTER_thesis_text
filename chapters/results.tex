\chapter{Results}
\label{chp:results}
This chapter presents the results of the classroom deployment described in Chapter~\ref{chp:experimental-setup}. It first establishes that the collected data is complete and reliable enough to draw conclusions from, then examines how the adaptive system behaved in practice, the engagement data from the coin and puzzle system, the children's questionnaire responses, and finally the structured feedback from the researchers who used RekenRangers in the field.

\section{Data quality and completeness}
\label{sec:data-quality}
Across all four conditions, RekenRangers recorded 21,522 exercises from 102 participants. The original sample consisted of 107 participants, but five were excluded during data cleaning: four from condition A and one from condition B. The reason for exclusion was a bug discovered after the experiments, where participants who clicked the submit button very quickly could cause the same exercise to be recorded twice. Participants with a significant number of duplicate entries were removed; a few negligible duplicates in other participants were retained. The bug has since been fixed.

Not every participant completed all four training days; some children were absent on individual days due to illness or other reasons. Whether this matters depends on the analysis. Where each exercise is treated as an independent record, missing days do not affect the result. For analyses that depend on a participant's full training history, such as the Elo trajectories, participants without all four days were excluded. On one occasion, the RekenRangers server was temporarily unavailable when a session was scheduled; the session was rescheduled and no data was lost.

The CSV export functioned as designed and could be loaded directly into data analysis frameworks without preprocessing, beyond the duplicate removal described above. One minor issue: server timestamps are offset due to a server-clock timezone setting, but this affects no analysis, since response times are measured and stored locally in the browser. Of the 102 participants, 51 completed the questionnaire described in Section~\ref{sec:questionnaire}; those results are presented in Section~\ref{sec:questionnaire-results}.

\section{The adaptive system in practice}
The adaptive difficulty system described in Section~\ref{sec:adaptivity} was used in two of the four experimental conditions (C and D). The non-adaptive conditions (A and B) presented exercises drawn from a balanced mix of all three difficulty levels in a semi-random order. This section examines how the adaptive system behaved in practice using both quantitative data and qualitative observations.

\subsection{Elo trajectories}
Figure~\ref{fig:elo-trajectories} shows the Elo trajectories of a larger sample of participants from the adaptive conditions over the course of their 216 exercises. All children started at the initial rating of 450, but their paths diverge quickly, and four broad patterns become visible across the sample.

First, the ceiling of 1000 was reached: a number of the strongest participants climbed rapidly during the first session and stayed at the ceiling for the rest of the training period. Second, and more striking, a sizeable subgroup of participants got stuck around a rating of 600. This value sits on the boundary between difficulty zones 3 and 4 (see Table~\ref{tab:difficulty-zones}), and the implications of this observation are discussed in detail in Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion}. Third, the trajectories are not smooth curves but jagged paths with frequent ups and downs, which is expected behaviour: the Elo system responds to every exercise, so a single mistake produces an immediate dip. The overall trend remains clearly readable in each case. Fourth, not every participant ended up at either the ceiling or the 600 plateau. A range of intermediate trajectories is also visible, which means the system is capturing real individual differences in ability rather than pushing everyone toward the same point.

\subsection{Final Elo distribution}
Figure~\ref{fig:elo-distribution} shows the distribution of final Elo scores across all participants in the adaptive conditions. The distribution spans from approximately 500 to 1000 and contains two distinct peaks. The first peak sits around 600--650 and is consistent with the pattern observed in the trajectories: a sizeable group of participants ended up oscillating around the boundary between difficulty zones 3 and 4, with their final rating reflecting the side of the boundary they happened to land on. The second peak is at the maximum rating of 1000 and represents children who reached the ceiling and stayed there. For these children, the exercise pool could no longer offer sufficient challenge, as all exercises were already drawn from the highest difficulty level.

\subsection{Accuracy rates}
Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy-distribution} compares the distribution of per-student accuracy rates between the adaptive and non-adaptive conditions. The non-adaptive group achieved a mean accuracy of 76\% (\(\sigma = 0.16\)), which sits close to the 75\% target. The adaptive group achieved a lower mean accuracy of 68\% (\(\sigma = 0.14\)). The difference is expected: children in the adaptive conditions faced a higher average exercise difficulty (mean difficulty level of 2.54, compared to 2.00 in the non-adaptive conditions, where difficulty levels were balanced by design). The adaptive system pushed stronger children toward harder exercises, which increased the error rate. However, the adaptive system did not converge on the intended 75\% target. The mean accuracy of 68\% indicates that the current settings produced a difficulty level that was somewhat too challenging for the average participant. Why this happened, and what it would take to bring the system closer to its target, is examined in Section~\ref{sec:interpretation-of-results}.

\subsection{Response times}
Figure~\ref{fig:response-time-distribution} shows the distribution of mean response times per student for both groups. The non-adaptive group has a mean response time of 9.5 seconds (\(\sigma = 2.11\)) and shows a wide, relatively uniform spread. This is consistent with the fact that these children received exercises from all three difficulty levels regardless of their ability: strong children solved easy exercises quickly while weaker children spent longer on the harder ones, producing a flatter distribution. The adaptive group has a higher mean response time of 11.7 seconds (\(\sigma = 1.96\)) and a narrower spread concentrated toward the longer end. This is consistent with how the scoring function works: its equilibrium point sits at the time limit, so the adaptive algorithm tends to push children toward exercises that take them somewhat longer to solve.

\subsection{Qualitative observations}
The quantitative patterns are accompanied by observations made during the classroom sessions. In their feedback report, the researchers noted that the non-adaptive group showed visible signs of frustration or boredom more frequently than the adaptive group. They also noted, particularly at one of the schools, that several children in the adaptive conditions asked why their exercises were so difficult, and that these children were all pupils who had not repeated a grade.

\section{Engagement}
\subsection{Coins and puzzles}
A central question of this thesis is whether gamification elements help keep children engaged across multiple sessions. The coin and puzzle system in RekenRangers offers a way to examine this with behavioural data rather than self-report: if children spend their coins on puzzles, they are engaging with the gamification system, and if they complete puzzles, they are treating the animal rescue mission as a genuine subgoal.

Table~\ref{tab:coin-summary} summarises the coin and puzzle data across the four conditions. The results are striking: across the 102 participants, children spent on average 98.5\% of their earned coins on puzzle pieces, and none spent less than 85\%. The pattern holds across all four conditions, regardless of whether the condition included metacognitive prompts or adaptive difficulty. Children were never instructed to spend their coins: the puzzle shop was available at the end of each training day and using it was entirely optional, with no check or reward attached. The small number of unspent coins per child (on average 0 to 25 coins) is mostly explained by leftover coins being insufficient to buy another puzzle piece.

The puzzle completion data tells a similar story. Across all conditions, 99\% of children completed at least one puzzle, and 44\% completed three or more over the four training days. The coin economy was deliberately calibrated so that completing all six puzzles within 216 exercises was impossible, ensuring there was always a next goal to work toward. The data also shows different purchasing strategies: some children prioritised cheaper puzzles to rescue as many animals as possible, while others saved for more expensive puzzles and completed fewer.

The mean number of coins earned was higher in the non-adaptive conditions (A and B, around 665 coins) than in the adaptive conditions (C and D, around 615 coins). This follows from the adaptive system presenting harder exercises to stronger children, which lengthens response times and reduces the speed bonus. The spending percentages, however, were highly similar across all four conditions.

\subsection{Within-day consistency}
\label{sec:within-day-consistency}
A second way to look at engagement is to ask how stable performance stays within a training day. Section~\ref{sec:bellon-studies} showed that in a comparable OpenSesame training, children answered progressively faster while becoming less accurate as the day went on, a pattern consistent with disengagement. The same view can be applied to the RekenRangers data. Each training day consisted of three exercise blocks of 18 exercises (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}), giving twelve blocks across the four days, labelled S1 to S12 in the figures.
 
Figure~\ref{fig:within-day-no-adaptivity} shows the average correctness and response time per block for the non-adaptive conditions (A and B), the closest match to the no-adaptivity setup in Section~\ref{sec:bellon-studies}. What stands out is mainly what does not happen. Correctness stays in a fairly narrow band across all twelve blocks, without the steady within-day decline that marked the OpenSesame data in Section~\ref{sec:bellon-studies}. Response times drop over the first three blocks and then settle, staying roughly flat for the rest of the training. Though the experiments differ too much to allow a formal comparison, the data suggests that the RekenRangers blocks are more consistent than Baeten's, with less sign of the issues identified in Section \ref{sec:bellon-studies}. The interpretation of these patterns is taken up in Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion}.

\section{Researcher feedback}
After the classroom deployments, Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems provided structured written feedback on their experience using RekenRangers as a research tool.\footnote{Both researchers used RekenRangers for their joint master's thesis in pedagogical sciences, and submitted their feedback together in a single document. Because both were involved in the development process and provided input on the tool's design throughout, their perspective is that of informed insiders rather than first-time users. This should be taken into account when interpreting the feedback.} The feedback is organised around four aspects: setting up an experiment, running it, monitoring it, and the quality of the collected data, followed by a set of additional suggestions.

On \textbf{setting up an experiment}, the researchers reported that configuring the study in RekenRangers was straightforward once the available options were understood. The initial learning curve was comparable to any new tool, and the manual helped navigate the interface. Compared to their experience with PsychoPy, RekenRangers made the setup process less complex: after consulting the literature to decide on the desired settings, the configuration could be done by selecting options rather than writing code. They also noted that the tool gives a clear warning when combinations of settings are incompatible and would generate insufficient exercises, though they suggested that the message could be more specific about which setting is causing the conflict.

On \textbf{running the experiment}, the researchers described the experience as a clear improvement over OpenSesame. They reported that OpenSesame regularly produced errors when opening tasks, particularly after software updates, and that these errors were time-consuming to resolve. By contrast, RekenRangers was easy to start and rarely showed errors. When an issue did occur, it was quickly resolved and the tool continued without further problems. They did note a minor bug where the settings had to be re-saved before each training day, and that the interactive demo was too long for the children, particularly the version that included the metacognitive explanation, causing visible drops in enthusiasm.

On \textbf{monitoring the experiment}, the researchers found it useful that they could check individual participant data directly within the tool through the results view, without needing to download the full dataset first. They suggested that the set identifiers in the export could be numbered per participant (e.g., 1 through 12) rather than using global database identifiers, which would make it easier to see at a glance which training day a particular record belongs to. They also suggested adding a live progress view so that a teacher or researcher could see in real time how far each child is in their exercise sequence.

On \textbf{data quality}, the researchers assessed the collected data as good, with the main concern being the duplicate entries caused by the submission bug described in Section~\ref{sec:data-quality}. They highlighted the convenience of being able to download the data directly from the researcher's account in the browser, without needing to transfer files between devices, and noted that knowing the data was stored server-side and could be recovered in case of problems was reassuring.

Finally, the researchers offered several \textbf{additional suggestions}: displaying the logged-in child's name on screen after login to help verify that each child is working on the correct account, showing the number of participants per group on the dashboard to help balance conditions, and giving the RekenRangers logo a place on the front page.
