\chapter{Experimental Setup}
\label{chp:experimental-setup}
The previous chapter described what RekenRangers is and how it addresses the twelve properties identified in Chapters \ref{chp:background} and \ref{chp:exercise-tools}. The question that remains is whether the tool actually delivers on these promises when used in a real classroom with real children. This chapter describes how that question was tested.

RekenRangers was deployed as the central training tool in a study conducted by Eline T'joen and Kiara Willems as part of their joint master's thesis in pedagogical sciences at KU Leuven. The study involved 146 third-grade children (birth year 2017) across seven primary schools in Flanders, working with the platform over four training days in four different experimental conditions. Due to time limitations on the analysis side, the dataset used in this thesis was restricted to 107 of these participants. This deployment serves as the primary evaluation of RekenRangers: it tests whether the tool can support a real multi-session experiment while producing complete and reliable data (RQ2), and the questionnaire administered after the final session captures how the children experienced the platform while also providing useful feedback on the tool itself (RQ3). The details of the classroom deployment are described in Section~\ref{sec:deployment}, and the design of the questionnaire in Section~\ref{sec:questionnaire}.

\section{Classroom deployments}
\label{sec:deployment}
The study, supervised by Dr. Elien Bellon and co-supervised by Prof. Dr. Bert De Smedt, investigated the effect of adaptive arithmetic training with metacognitive monitoring on the arithmetic skills and metacognitive abilities of third-grade children (ages 8--9). The participating schools were located in the provinces of Limburg and West-Flanders, and the data was collected during the spring of 2026.

Each condition involved four training days, with three exercise blocks of 18 exercises per day, totalling 54 exercises per session and 216 exercises across the full training period. All conditions used a mix of addition and multiplication, with an equal distribution of both operations by the end of each training day. The timer was hidden in all conditions. In the non-adaptive conditions (A and B), exercises were drawn in a semi-random order from a balanced mix of all three difficulty levels, with the constraint that no more than two consecutive exercises could come from the same difficulty level. In the adaptive conditions (C and D), the Elo-based system described in Section~\ref{sec:adaptivity} determined the difficulty distribution.

The research settings in RekenRangers were configured identically across all conditions: a fixation screen was shown before each exercise, identical exercises were blocked within a session, tie problems were excluded, commutativity was enforced, the position of the larger operand was balanced, and the values 0, 1, 10, and 11 were excluded as operands. These settings ensured that the generated exercises did not contain inherent biases caused by naturally easier solution strategies, allowing the evaluation to focus more fairly on the intended experimental conditions.

Prior to the main data collection, both researchers conducted a pilot study with one child each. The pilot confirmed that the adaptive algorithm produced appropriate difficulty progression, that the demo was understandable, and that the overall experience was positively received. One pilot participant reached the maximum Elo rating of 1000, indicating a ceiling effect, but after consultation this was attributed to the participant being an outlier rather than a structural problem with the difficulty range.

In addition to the RekenRangers training, participants completed a battery of pre- and post-tests, administered through OpenSesame and on paper, covering arithmetic, spelling, reasoning, and metacognitive monitoring. These measures were used by the other researchers to assess whether training effects transferred to standardised assessments and are not analysed in this thesis. After the final training session, children filled in the questionnaire described in Section~\ref{sec:questionnaire}.

\section{The questionnaire}
\label{sec:questionnaire}
After the final session, each participating child filled in a short paper-based questionnaire about their experience with RekenRangers. The goal was to collect structured feedback on four aspects of the tool, each corresponding to a property defined earlier in this thesis: whether children found it motivating (engagement), whether they could use it without difficulty (user friendliness), whether it kept them engaged (engagement), and whether the feedback and difficulty felt appropriate (feedback and adaptivity). The seven items are listed in Table~\ref{tab:questionnaire}.

Designing a questionnaire for children aged eight to nine years old imposes specific constraints. Questions must be short, concrete, and free of abstract formulations, and the response scale must be simple enough that a child can use it without hesitation. Research on surveying children confirms that these are methodological necessities, not just practical preferences: younger respondents are more sensitive to question complexity, and poorly formulated items produce unreliable data \cite{borgers2000children}.

The questionnaire uses a 4-point Likert scale, where one star means ``strongly disagree'' and four stars mean ``strongly agree''. A 4-point scale was chosen to discourage neutral midpoint responses and encourage clearer preference indications. The stars are presented visually on the paper form, making the scale intuitive without requiring explanation beyond a brief legend at the top of the page.

Three of the seven items are deliberately phrased in the negative direction. This is a standard technique in questionnaire design to detect ``acquiescence bias'', the tendency of respondents to agree with every statement regardless of its content \cite{podsakoff2003common}. If a child gives four stars to both ``I enjoyed using RekenRangers'' and ``I would not want to use RekenRangers again'', this signals inattentive or biased responding and allows the researcher to flag that response for closer inspection.

The items were not taken from an existing validated instrument, but are informed by established research traditions. The user friendliness items in particular reflect the core concerns of the System Usability Scale \cite{brooke1996sus}, simplified substantially to be appropriate for young children. The full questionnaire is included in Appendix~\ref{app:questionnaire}.
